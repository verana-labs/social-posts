The v5 draft of the [Verifiable Public Registry (VPR) specification](https://verana-labs.github.io/verifiable-trust-vpr-spec/) is published and open for review. It is the result of 357 commits, five review rounds and a full audit.

A trust registry answers a question that sounds simple: who is authorized to issue, verify or govern credentials in a given context? Across the industry, in government-led initiatives and in standards bodies alike, the work on trust registries keeps arriving at the same set of harder questions. Here is how the main features of the VPR answer them.

## Who is authorized? Accreditations

Issuers, verifiers and grantors are accredited per credential schema. Accreditations form a tree, from the ecosystem down to each participant, and every one of them has an effective window. A relying party can ask whether an issuer was accredited when a credential was issued, not only whether it is accredited today.

## Who decides? Each ecosystem

The VPR is a registry of registries. Every ecosystem publishes its own credential schemas and governance framework, and chooses how participants are onboarded: openly, against a credential, by the ecosystem itself, or through grantors it delegates to. The registry stores registrations, not decisions. Trust decisions stay where they belong: with the relying party.

## Who governs the registry itself? A council

A council of organizations is the sole authority for every governance decision, upgrade and parameter change of the network. Governance frameworks are not documents that sit on a website: their acceptance is recorded, at network, ecosystem and corporation level, and required before a party can act.

## Who operates it? No single operator

The VPR is public, decentralized infrastructure. It is agnostic to DID methods, and each schema declares its credential format: W3C Verifiable Credentials, AnonCreds, SD-JWT or mdoc. No vendor and no single authority sits in the middle.

## What if a trusted party misbehaves? Accountability

Being listed is not the end of the story. Sanctions and exclusions are decided by the ecosystem, and they bind the corporation in that ecosystem: it cannot simply come back under another identifier, schema or role.

## Can it be audited? Exchange records

Credential issuance and credential presentation are recorded as two distinct kinds of events. Where a schema asks for it, a digest of the credential is anchored, never the credential itself.

## Built in the open

These questions are not ours alone. They are on the table at the Global Digital Collaboration Trust Registries Task Force and at Trust over IP, two communities the Verana Foundation takes part in. The Verana indexer implements Trust over IP's Trust Registry Query Protocol (TRQP v2), so a relying party can query the registry in a registry-agnostic way.

## What comes next

v5 is a draft: v4 remains the latest stable version. The companion Verifiable Trust specification v5 is in review, and the implementations follow. Transfer of a service between corporations and schema authorization policies are not part of this version.

### LinkedIn (personal)

Every room working on trust registries ends up at the same two questions: who is authorized to issue what, and who gets to decide?

The v5 draft of the Verifiable Public Registry (VPR) specification is published and open for review. 357 commits, five review rounds and a full audit went into it. Here is how its main features line up with what the industry is trying to solve.

1. Who is authorized? Accreditations. Issuers, verifiers and grantors are accredited per credential schema, in a tree, each with an effective window. You can ask whether an issuer was accredited when a credential was issued, not only whether it is today.

2. Who decides? Each ecosystem. The VPR is a registry of registries: every ecosystem brings its own schemas, governance framework and onboarding rules. The registry stores registrations, not decisions. Trust decisions stay with the relying party.

3. Who governs the registry itself? A council. A council of organizations is the sole authority for governance decisions, upgrades and parameters. And acceptance of governance frameworks is recorded and required before anyone can act.

4. Who operates it? No single operator. Public, decentralized infrastructure, agnostic to DID methods, with the credential format declared per schema: W3C VC, AnonCreds, SD-JWT or mdoc.

5. What if a trusted party misbehaves? Accountability. Sanctions and exclusions are decided by the ecosystem and bind the corporation there, under any identifier, schema or role.

6. Can it be audited? Issuance and presentation leave distinct records. Digests are anchored, never credentials.

None of these questions are ours alone. They are on the table at the Global Digital Collaboration Trust Registries Task Force and at Trust over IP, where we take part, and our indexer speaks ToIP's Trust Registry Query Protocol (TRQP v2).
